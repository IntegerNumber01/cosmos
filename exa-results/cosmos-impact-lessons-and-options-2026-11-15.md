# COSMOS: impact lessons from winners, citizen science and the forums

Research date: 15 November 2026. Sources reviewed: 40 search results requested across five Exa searches, followed by targeted full-page reads. This count includes duplicate search results; it is not 40 independent projects. This is a focused comparison, not an exhaustive census of winners or unpublished judging decisions.

## Recommendation

Make COSMOS a practical introduction to investigating change in real astronomical observations. Start with students and astronomy-club beginners, with teachers and club facilitators as the people who can bring it to groups. Keep the full tools available for experienced amateur astronomers.

The immediate outcome should be specific: a learner compares real observations, distinguishes a supported brightness change from an inconclusive or misleading result, explains one remaining uncertainty, and saves evidence another person can inspect. The advanced outcome is a reproducible investigation worth discussing with a knowledgeable reviewer. We have not established that these outcomes occur for real users yet.

The strongest next step is a short, tested investigation kit using existing measurements and safeguards. Extra features alone will not establish impact. Equally, a 48-hour prototype does not need a large existing user base to have a credible impact case. The official rubric considers the significance, quality, reach and potential of the solution; user observations are useful supporting evidence, not a separate eligibility requirement. [Official judging guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/judging-awards-guide/)

## What previous winners actually show

The official announcements verify awards and describe proposed solutions. They generally do not report controlled educational evaluations, adoption figures or private judges' reasons. The transfers below are our interpretations of the public record.

| Example | Publicly documented approach | Useful transfer to COSMOS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Diamond in the Sky**, Team Diamonds, 2022 Most Inspirational | An interactive learning game specifically for children aged 10–12, teaching stellar variability, patterns, colour and brightness. Its project page describes learning before play and progressively unlocked activities. | Astronomy education is a credible competition direction. Name a learner and a learning activity. For COSMOS, teach the interpretation of real observations rather than copy its simulated star-creation game. |
| **Selene**, 2022 Galactic Impact | A responsive 3D Moon application retrieves and visualizes moonquakes for a user-selected date. | A useful, understandable interface to scientific data can have an impact case. A grand claim about saving lives is unnecessary. The announcement does not demonstrate audience learning or explain the award decision. |
| **MIMBI**, 2022 Best Storytelling | QR codes lead to a web experience about Paraguay's first satellite, using local textures, rhymes, music and art. | Put the experience where a community can encounter it: a classroom handout or astronomy-club session with a link to the exact investigation. Distribution and context matter alongside functionality. |
| **Eco-Metropolis**, Innovisionaries, 2024 Most Inspirational | Players make city-planning and resource-management choices informed by environmental data. | Let learners make an evidence-based decision and explain the consequence. For us, the decision is whether the apparent change survives the available checks. |
| **G.R.O.W.**, Team I.O., 2024 Local Impact | Makes complex GIS information accessible through approachable environmental communication. | Explain the barrier we remove in one concrete workflow, and test whether non-specialists can complete it. Do not assume an attractive viewer has removed that barrier. |
| **2plant / !2plant**, NVS-knot, 2024 Galactic Impact | Uses soil moisture and evapotranspiration to help farmers assess planting risk. | Translate a dataset into a decision for an identified beneficiary. COSMOS's equivalent is a defensible interpretation and next observation, not just a colour image. |
| **Astro Sweepers**, 2025 Galactic Impact | Turns orbital data into debris-assessment reports and a risk index. | An inspectable output gives an investigation a purpose beyond browsing. COSMOS already exports evidence; test whether a second person can reconstruct and understand it. |
| **QUEÑARIS**, 2025 Local Impact | Focuses on water scarcity and queñua-forest restoration around Arequipa, Peru. | A local impact story should name an actual local setting and beneficiary. A lesson delivered to a nearby club is stronger evidence than an unsupported worldwide-reach claim. |

Sources: [2022 official winners](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/blog/2022-global-winners/), [Team Diamonds' original project](https://2022.spaceappschallenge.org/challenges/2022-challenges/twinkle-twinkle-little-star/teams/team-diamonds/project/), [NASA's 2024 winners announcement](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-international-space-apps-challenge-announces-2024-global-winners/), [NASA's 2025 winners announcement](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/).

Diamond in the Sky is particularly close thematically. Its existence means COSMOS should not claim that teaching stellar variability interactively is itself unprecedented. Our proposed distinction is the integrated investigation of dated SPHEREx observations, reference-star calibration, explicit refusal of unreliable measurements, and a worksheet that preserves unresolved explanations. This is a meaningful combination to demonstrate, not proof of being the first tool to offer any individual capability.

## What mature astronomy projects add

**Backyard Worlds: Planet 9** provides introductory training, real image flipbooks, a way to report candidate objects, and discussion with volunteers and scientists. NASA documents published scientific contributions and volunteer co-authorship. Its impact includes a route from observing to review and follow-up, not merely access to an image viewer. COSMOS can borrow the training-to-investigation structure now. A genuine scientific contribution programme would additionally need reviewers, an agreed submission process and follow-up capacity; none is established by our current export button. [NASA project overview](https://science.nasa.gov/citizen-science/backyard-worlds-planet-9/)

**Exoplanet Watch** welcomes beginners, offers data to participants without telescopes, provides analysis software and a community, and routes results into an established exoplanet database. NASA reports that participants had studied over 400 exoplanets and made over 6,000 light curves **as of March 2024**. That dated output count illustrates measurable participation; it is not an achievement COSMOS can inherit or a current total. The transferable idea is a clearly defined useful output, accessible starting material and somewhere appropriate for that output to go. [NASA Exoplanet Watch overview](https://exoplanets.nasa.gov/exoplanet-watch/about-exoplanet-watch/overview/)

For COSMOS, learning to reject an apparent change is a useful outcome even when no new object is discovered. A research-discovery impact claim would require substantially more evidence than our current benchmark and control results.

## Useful forum ideas and their limits

1. **Ask for a small, concrete trial.** A Lunar Landing Site Navigator team asks three to five people for a roughly ten-minute test, with no sign-up, and asks what confused them first. A Navigator supports that feedback approach. The thread we reviewed does not contain completed study results. We can reuse the low-friction recruitment pattern for a local screen-share test without deploying publicly. [Discussion #2092](https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/2092)
2. **Explain who benefits and the practical value.** Pratik, identifying himself as Astro Sweepers' 2025 winning team lead and a 2026 Navigator, emphasizes problem framing, beneficiaries, applications and a clear demonstration. NASA's winner announcement corroborates the team and award. This is relevant experience, not an extra judging rule. [Discussion #1988](https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/1988)
3. **Use one complete beneficiary story.** In a Navigator introduction thread, participant `sarthaksarm` suggests moving from the affected person and problem to NASA data, a short demonstration and potential impact. That particular advice is a participant's reply, not a statement by the former judge who opened the thread. [Discussion #1990](https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/1990)
4. **Check claims about existing viewers.** A SPHEREx thread asks why another tool is needed. A respondent suggests approachable temporal and multi-band investigation. This is a useful product question, but the respondent's authority is not established by the fetched page, and its claims about what existing tools cannot do should not be repeated uncritically. IRSA already supplies SPHEREx tools, and WiseView is an important precedent. Our value needs to be demonstrated in the integrated workflow. [Discussion #2217](https://github.com/nasa/spaceapps/discussions/2217), [IRSA SPHEREx hub](https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/Missions/spherex.html)

No forum messages were posted and no people were contacted during this research.

## Ranked ideas for COSMOS

Effort estimates are relative implementation judgments, not measured development commitments. All new activities below are proposals. Existing lessons, controls and exports reduce their effort but do not establish audience benefit.

| Priority / idea | Audience and useful outcome | What we already have / proposed addition | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. A 15–20 minute real-sky investigation kit** | Students and club beginners complete a defensible investigation; a facilitator can run it. | Existing RW Aur lesson, prepared observations, calibration and worksheet. Add a one-page facilitator guide, prerequisites, timing and an example evidence-based answer. Low effort. | Completion without coaching; ability to explain the two dates, calibrated change, uncertainty and next check; facilitator's account of whether it fits a session. |
| **2. “Would you believe this change?” activity** | Learners recognize that a dramatic number can be misleading. | Use RW Aur, Epsilon Indi B's no-significant-change result, and the blended-star control that formerly produced −54% at 18σ. Make the rejected control directly inspectable with its provenance if it is included as a learner case. Ask for reasoning before revealing the explanation. Medium effort. | Can learners distinguish supported, inconclusive and refused results, explain at least one failure mechanism, and apply that reasoning to a different example? |
| **3. Reproducible handoff to a club reviewer** | An interested amateur gives a mentor enough information to check an investigation. | Existing share URLs, notes and JSON export. Add a concise human-readable summary only if users find the JSON difficult: target, dates, wavelengths, calibration, flags, unresolved checks and source links. Low–medium effort. | A second person reopens the same comparison and identifies why the conclusion is limited. Record what information they still need. |
| **4. A classroom session resilient to archive outages** | Facilitators can finish a prepared activity when IRSA or a catalogue service is unavailable. | Prepared cutouts, calibration fields and explicit service failures already help. Label which activities depend on live services and test the selected lesson with those services blocked. Low effort. | Does the selected activity remain usable and scientifically honest during a simulated outage? This is prepared-data resilience, not a claim that the entire site works offline. |
| **5. One repeatable astronomy-club session** | A specific local group gets a usable activity it can repeat. | Package the first two ideas with an exact link or QR code and a facilitator handout. Ask one available educator or club lead to review it. Low effort, requires willing humans. | Actual participant count, tasks completed, misconceptions found, facilitator feedback and whether they elect to reuse it. An invitation is not a partnership or adoption. |
| **6. Beginner-to-advanced progression** | A learner can start simply and an experienced user can inspect the science. | Existing `/learn`, collapsible technical details and full tools. Refine the transition using observed confusion; offer an optional GP Mon comparison after a beginner activity. Low–medium effort. | Beginners interpret the result without being overwhelmed; experienced users reach source data and measurement limits without forced lessons. |
| **7. Measure the investigation barrier** | Establish whether the proposed workflow saves effort for the intended task. | Compare the same narrowly defined task using COSMOS and an existing archive workflow with comparable participants and a consistent help policy. Medium effort. | Actual task completion, elapsed time and interpretation accuracy. Account for prior experience and order effects. Do not claim “hours to seconds” or superiority from a single coached demo. |
| **8. Reviewed candidate programme, later** | Trained volunteers could eventually contribute credible follow-up candidates. | Would require an actual astronomy collaborator, review rules, queue, false-alarm policy, attribution and follow-up route. High effort; defer. | Reviewer agreement, useful reports accepted, false alarms and confirmed follow-up outcomes. A large discovery counter would not substitute for this process. |

The recommended bundle is **1 + 2 + a small real pilot**, followed by handoff improvements if the pilot exposes a problem. Do not build all eight simply to fill the feature list. Keep motion and coordinate-driven exploration visible so the educational story continues to answer the actual SPHEREx challenge. [Official challenge](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/planet-x-and-spherex/)

## A small pilot we can actually report

Use the existing `docs/USER_TEST.md` protocol with three to five willing participants if available. A smaller sample can still reveal problems; report its size explicitly.

Before introducing the explanation, ask how they would decide whether two images show a real change. Let them complete the prepared investigation, record all help, and ask them to explain a different case. A brief before/after question is formative evidence of understanding in this session, not a validated learning-gain study. Keep an “inconclusive” answer available; avoiding overclaiming is part of the intended skill.

Record anonymous participant IDs, experience, device, selected case, task completion, time, explanation accuracy, confusion and service failures. Use actual counts with denominators. Ask what was confusing and what they would do next. Ask a facilitator separately whether they could use the kit and what preparation it requires. An answer that it looks appealing is weaker evidence than someone successfully using it.

Optional recruitment draft for the team to share themselves:

> We are testing COSMOS, a browser tool for comparing real infrared sky observations. Could you try a roughly 15-minute investigation over screen share? No astronomy background is needed. We want to see what is confusing and whether the evidence makes sense. We are testing the tool, not you; we can record anonymous task notes without your name or a recording.

This text is a draft only. Do not claim a NASA partnership, request discovery submissions to a nonexistent programme, or imply that a public deployment is already available.

## A defensible impact statement now

“COSMOS is designed to help students and citizen scientists investigate how the infrared sky changes using real SPHEREx observations. Guided comparisons, reference-star measurements and an evidence worksheet help users examine a possible change, recognize false alarms and keep a reproducible record. Our next validation step is a small learner and facilitator pilot.”

This describes an intended benefit and existing capabilities. After testing, add the actual observed outcome and sample size. Do not replace the final sentence with invented learning gains, worldwide reach, proven ease of use or confirmed new discoveries.

## How the direction supports the judging criteria

- **Impact:** names a beneficiary, a practical activity, an output and a way to test whether it helps; local delivery can support a credible community story.
- **Creativity:** makes investigation and the rejection of false positives part of the experience. The combination must be demonstrated without claiming that blinking, stellar-variability education or citizen science are new inventions.
- **Validity:** retains calibrated uncertainties, refused measurements, controls and unresolved checks. A user pilot also tests the usability dimension of validity.
- **Relevance:** the activity uses real dated SPHEREx observations, matching wavelengths and repeatable comparisons. The teaching explains the challenge workflow rather than replacing it with unrelated space trivia.

These are qualitative links to the rubric, not predicted judges' scores. Presentation remains an additional criterion in the official guide. No slides were created or changed for this work.
