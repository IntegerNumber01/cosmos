# COSMOS: short formative user test

Status: **protocol prepared; no human participants tested by this change**. Browser regressions verify behavior but are not evidence of audience appeal, learning or adoption.

## Purpose

Check whether a person new to astronomy can use COSMOS to investigate a real change, interpret its limits and keep the evidence. Also check that experienced users can reach the full tools without completing a tutorial.

Use three to five willing participants for an initial small study. Seek a mix of interested beginners, students or educators, and at least one amateur astronomer if available. Explain that the product is being tested, not the participant. Use anonymous participant IDs; no names, recordings or tracking are needed. Do not put likenesses or voices of anyone under 18 in submission materials.

## Give the participant these tasks

Start from the homepage. Do not demonstrate the workflow first.

1. Find a real example of something changing in the sky. Compare the two dates.
2. Tell me what changed and what the measurements say. What do the colours on screen represent?
3. Find one reason why the result might be misleading or uncertain.
4. Find whether the object is already known. If a service is unavailable, explain what that means.
5. Save the measurements or share the exact comparison so another person can check your answer.

For an experienced participant: open the full tools, find the sky-coordinate input, change a date selection, and find the native measurements, flags and original source links. Opening an advanced control is not a requirement to understand every scientific method.

Ask the participant to think aloud if comfortable. Record help whenever supplied. A broken archive or catalogue is an observation about the experience; do not silently substitute a fabricated success.

Use a timer from the homepage until they can explain the first result. Record the selected target and dates, device, viewport and service failures. Distinguish completing a click sequence from understanding the evidence.

Follow up: What was confusing? What would you do next? Would this fit an astronomy lesson or personal investigation? Do not lead participants toward a positive answer.

## Interpret honestly

Questions to resolve before testing, not scores to invent afterward:

- Can most participants reach and interpret the first comparison without coaching?
- Can they distinguish display colours from an object's visible appearance?
- Can they distinguish a prediction from an observed position and uncertainty from certainty?
- Do they understand that no catalogue match, or a failed lookup, does not establish a discovery?
- Can they save or share evidence, and can experienced users find the full controls?

Summarize actual counts (for example, “3 of 4 completed task 1 without help”) and observed times. Report the sample size and limitations. Do not claim worldwide adoption, validated learning gains, or comparison with archive tools from this small formative study.

Keep observations and resulting product fixes alongside each other. Repeat only tasks affected by an observed failure or a subsequent change.
